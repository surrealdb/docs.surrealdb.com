Watching a room full of young students go quiet over a science broadcast is a fine sight, kookaburra99. I dare say moments like this stay with a youngster for decades. I still recall gathering by the wireless as a lad, hanging on every crackle of static.

Down at JPL they are passing round the lucky peanuts, I see. A harmless bit of tradition dating back to the Ranger Moon probes in '64. Back on the night shift we had our own superstitions - one fellow insisted on placing a clean punch card on top of the card reader before every monthly ledger run. It did not alter the circuits, of course, but it kept everyone calm while the machinery got on with the job.

Now we are only minutes away from the top of the Martian atmosphere. Direct line of sight to Earth will drop away shortly, and everything hinges on Odyssey picking up the automated descent tones. Pacing will not help, so we just wait quietly for the signal.

Walter

2. Powered descent stage ignition on the retrorockets to brake the vehicle.
3. The sky crane descent, lowering the rover on bridles before severed cords allow the stage to fly clear.

All of this has already occurred fourteen minutes ago in reality, so we are merely watching historical data confirm whether the onboard guidance executed autonomously without a fault. It is fascinating to watch the Odyssey link hold its lock so reliably, but we must wait for the actual touchdown telemetry before drawing any conclusions about the vehicle's integrity.

In short: excellent progress on the descent software so far, but the most delicate mechanical manoeuvre is still ahead of us.
