PintOfMild wrote:getting the beast down in one piece is half the battle!
Equating the mechanical descent with the scientific mission is a fundamental error. An intact rover that suffers an avionics failure, a jammed drill mechanism, or contaminated sample chambers would merely represent a very expensive piece of scrap iron on the Martian regolith. The landing is an engineering prerequisite, nothing more. Its scientific merit rests entirely upon whether the instruments can reliably extract and process empirical data over the coming years. Planning pub visits before a single packet of scientific telemetry has even been verified seems remarkably premature.

NewsFri Aug 3, 2012 11:17 am GMT · Pasadena, United StatesSteltzner: the sky crane is 'the least crazy' optionrecalled by 4 members

Landing chief Adam Steltzner told reporters that the sky crane looks a little crazy but is the least crazy way to land a rover as big as Curiosity. Legs, as used by Viking and Phoenix, would make such a large rover unstable, and it is too heavy for airbags. The plan: hit the atmosphere at about 21,000 km/h, open a parachute about 11 km up, slow to about 320 km/h, then use rockets to slow to under 3 km/h before lowering the rover on cables. Source

Re: Curiosity lands on Mars on Monday morning - who's watching?

by quasistatic » Fri Aug 3, 2012 11:48 am

No misery intended on my part, Kev. A celebratory drink is harmless enough, provided we remain clear on the science.

Drilling into Martian rock is hardly a walk in the park, either. Curiosity carries a 2.1-metre robotic arm and a drill designed to penetrate 2.5 centimetres into the surface to deliver powder directly to its internal ovens and spectrometers. We have never attempted that depth before with an autonomous roving vehicle. The mechanical stresses on those tools in an extreme thermal environment are non-trivial.

Getting down safely will certainly be an immense engineering milestone, but we ought not to underestimate the complexity of what follows.

NewsFri Aug 3, 2012 12:00 pm GMT · Darmstadt, GermanyEurope's Mars Express will listen in on the landingrecalled by 1 member

The European Space Agency says its Mars Express orbiter will record Curiosity's radio signals during the descent, from 07:09 to 07:37 CEST on 6 August (05:09-05:37 UTC), as a backup to NASA's own tracking. ESA's New Norcia station in Australia will also listen for the signals directly. The recordings will be sent to JPL straight away. Mars Express images also helped NASA shrink Curiosity's landing ellipse to about 20 x 7 km. Source

NewsFri Aug 3, 2012 1:42 pm GMT · Washington, United StatesNASA gives $1.1 billion to private space taxi firmsnot recalled by anyone

NASA has awarded $1.1 billion to three companies to develop spacecraft to carry astronauts: Boeing gets $460 million, SpaceX $440 million and Sierra Nevada $212.5 million for its Dream Chaser space plane. Since the space shuttle retired last year, NASA has depended on Russian Soyuz flights to the space station, at about $63 million a seat. Source

NewsFri Aug 3, 2012 2:57 pm GMT · Pasadena, United StatesCuriosity team will live on Mars timerecalled by 3 members

For the first 90 days after landing, Curiosity's controllers will work on Martian time, with each day about 24 hours and 40 minutes long. Deputy project scientist Ashwin Vasavada says this gets the most out of the new rover. Most images will come home through relays by the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and Mars Odyssey, each of which can talk to the rover twice a day. Source

NewsFri Aug 3, 2012 4:09 pm GMT · Pasadena, United StatesWho writes Curiosity's tweetsnot recalled by anyone

The @MarsCuriosity Twitter account, written in the rover's own voice, is run by three women led by NASA social media manager Veronica McGregor. It started in November 2008 and has more than 137,000 followers. The rover's name came from 12-year-old Clara Ma, whose essay won a student contest in 2009 from more than 9,000 entries. Source
