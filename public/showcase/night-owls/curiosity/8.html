The broader view of the crater rim and the gravel field is certainly pleasing, PintOfMild, but Walter and OffByOne have it right.

To keep our thinking clear, we must separate the visual confirmations from the engineering reality. A crisp photograph of pebbles tells us only that the optics survived the descent stage; it tells us nothing about the health of the internal sensors or sample handling mechanisms. Declaring the expenditure justified before those subsequent telemetry passes are complete would be entirely premature.

We await the hard diagnostic logs, nothing less.

kookaburra99 wrote:sorry if this is a dumb question, but do we get those radiation numbers straight away after the five hours, or do we have to wait for another pass?
Actually, the high-gain antenna does not provide instant streaming to the public. Curiosity buffers its sensor measurements locally, meaning those radiation logs must await scheduled orbiter relay passes or dedicated Deep Space Network tracking windows.

While the release of the descent imager footage and the hazard camera views of Mount Sharp is certainly encouraging, pictures do not verify calibration baselines. The mission value cannot be established by mere visual evidence. We must examine the raw engineering telemetry from the forthcoming downlink cycles before drawing conclusions regarding the operational viability of the analytical payload. Geduld is required.
