■ Posted: Wed Aug 16, 2006 6:18 am    Post subject: Re: Astronomers vote on what a planet is - is Pluto safe?quote
The draft text has just been reported this morning by Space.com, and it is rather extraordinary. The committee proposes that any object orbiting a star is a planet provided it has sufficient self-gravity to be round, and is not itself a satellite.

Under that rule, the immediate count rises to twelve. Ceres becomes a planet again, as does 2003 UB313, and remarkably, Charon is included because the barycentre of the Pluto-Charon system sits in open space between them rather than inside Pluto.

This completely sidesteps the dynamical question. Instead of asking whether an object has cleared its orbital zone, the panel has chosen an entirely hydrostatic criterion. Mike Brown has already described the result as a complete mess, pointing out that dozens more bodies in the Kuiper belt will qualify as soon as their shapes are confirmed, whereas Alan Stern supports it. Alan Boss remarked that it reads as though it were drafted by lawyers rather than scientists, and I must say I see his point. It looks far more like a political compromise to keep Pluto than a sensible physical classification. We shall see how the wider assembly receives it.

■ Posted: Wed Aug 16, 2006 6:24 am    Post subject: Re: Astronomers vote on what a planet is - is Pluto safe?quote
Strictly speaking, 2003 UB313 cannot yet be called a planet in any formal sense until the assembly actually ratifies this text next week.

That said, I find the published wording deeply disappointing. By relying entirely on hydrostatic equilibrium - essentially whether self-gravity has pulled a mass into a nearly round shape - the committee has constructed a purely geophysical threshold while ignoring orbital dynamics altogether. A genuine astronomical classification cannot simply disregard how a body interacts with its orbital environment. Alan Boss is quite right that this reads like an unscientific legal compromise, drafted specifically to preserve Pluto's historical status without explicitly stating so.

If roundness alone suffices, Mike Brown's observation that dozens of additional trans-Neptunian objects will qualify is mathematically unavoidable. We are trading one historical anomaly for taxonomic chaos. A definition ought to clarify reality, not obscure it with artificial legalisms, and I suspect the wider assembly will find this compromise rather hard to stomach.

NewsWed Aug 16, 2006 7:00 am GMT · Prague, Czech RepublicIAU proposes 12 planets and a new class called 'plutons'recalled by 7 members

The IAU has released its draft definition. A planet is a body that is massive enough for its own gravity to make it nearly round, and that orbits a star without being a star or a satellite of a planet. If members approve it on 24 August, there will be 12 planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Ceres, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Pluto, Charon and 2003 UB313. Pluto stays a planet and becomes the model for a new class of 'plutons', planets that take more than 200 years to orbit the Sun. A dozen more candidates are on a watchlist. Committee chair Owen Gingerich says agreement came after a long day, 'the miracle had happened'. Source

NewsWed Aug 16, 2006 7:05 am GMT · Prague, Czech RepublicDraft footnotes make Charon a planet and set a size guiderecalled by 5 members

The full text of draft Resolution 5 says round bodies are usually those above 5 x 10^20 kg and 800 km across, with an IAU process for borderline cases. Where two bodies orbit a point outside the larger one, as Pluto and Charon do, both are planets, so Pluto-Charon would be a double planet. If Pallas, Vesta or Hygiea prove to be round, they would also be planets. The term 'minor planet' would no longer be used. Source

NewsWed Aug 16, 2006 7:30 am GMT · London, United KingdomNature asks readers for a 12-planet mnemonicrecalled by 1 member

Nature's news blog reports the IAU proposal under the headline that planets are round, and quotes the IAU's question-and-answer sheet: a pluton is a planet, Pluto is a pluton, so Pluto is a planet. It asks readers for a mnemonic for the new list of 12, since 'My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nine Pizzas' only covers nine. Source

■ Posted: Wed Aug 16, 2006 10:13 am    Post subject: Re: Astronomers vote on what a planet is - is Pluto safe?quote
The issue is that the proposed resolution creates a very messy precedent. By treating hydrostatic equilibrium as the sole determinant, the committee has effectively discarded the astronomical context in favour of geology alone.

Consider the practical ramifications of the footnotes. If shape alone qualifies a body, then Ceres is merely the first asteroid to re-enter the catalogue. Bodies such as Pallas, Vesta, and Hygiea are already noted as potential candidates should further observations show they achieved equilibrium. In the outer solar system, the situation becomes unmanageable very quickly. There are already dozens of trans-Neptunian objects with diameters suggesting sufficient mass for roundness. If we must elevate every distant ice ball to full planetary status simply because its gravity smoothed its edges, the term ceases to convey meaningful structural information about our solar system.

A sensible taxonomy must describe how a body dominates its neighbourhood. An asteroid belt or a swarm of Kuiper belt objects is a distinct dynamical phenomenon from a major planet. I suspect many planetary astronomers in Prague will see this draft for what it is, an unworkable administrative fix designed purely to placate sentimental attachments.
