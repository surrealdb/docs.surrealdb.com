■ Posted: Fri Aug 18, 2006 10:32 am    Post subject: Re: Astronomers vote on what a planet is - is Pluto safe?quote
It is reassuring to see a sensible technical appraisal take hold here. Pointing out that a drafted boundary condition produces odd results - like the Earth and Moon parting company on the charts billions of years hence - is precisely why these papers are circulated to the full membership before a ballot is called.

When a group sits down to craft a rule, they naturally concentrate on the problem right before their eyes. In this case, they were trying to accommodate Pluto, Ceres, and the newer discoveries without writing an endless catalogue of exceptions. That they overlooked an eccentric orbit or a drifting satellite is not evidence of a hidden scheme. It is merely the standard way drafting works. You propose a model, the specialists examine it for flaws, and then the text is refined to eliminate the nonsense.

Whether the final wording leans towards physical shape or dynamic mechanics, the assembly will sort it out through ordinary argument. We would do well to wait calmly and see what amendments emerge from Prague before deciding the matter is lost.

NewsFri Aug 18, 2006 2:00 pm GMT · Pasadena, United StatesMike Brown: the 'leave no iceball behind' definition will probably passnot recalled by anyone

On NPR's Science Friday, Mike Brown, who is not an IAU member and is not in Prague, says the round-body rule would eventually mean 100 or 200 planets; he counts about 43 known objects that qualify. He compares 'planet' to 'continent', a cultural word without a scientific definition, and would prefer either keeping nine or going to eight. He expects members to vote yes out of 'planet fatigue'. He admits it is exciting that one of the 12 is his discovery. Source

NewsFri Aug 18, 2006 2:57 pm GMT · Prague, Czech RepublicRival plan would make Pluto a 'dwarf planet'recalled by 3 members

At a meeting of the IAU's planetary scientists, a straw vote found only about 18 in favour of the official proposal, about 20 for reworking it, and about 50 for an alternative from Julio Angel Fernandez of Uruguay. That plan requires a planet to be by far the largest body in its region, which would leave eight planets and make Pluto a 'dwarf planet'. Owen Gingerich says letters to him are evenly split and that a dwarf planet which is not a planet is 'linguistically objectionable'. The IAU Executive Committee is to decide the next step. Source

■ Posted: Sat Aug 19, 2006 8:52 am    Post subject: Re: Astronomers vote on what a planet is - is Pluto safe?quote
Language often contains these apparent contradictions when technical classifications diverge from common speech. Owen Gingerich himself noted that a dwarf planet which is not a planet is linguistically awkward, but science frequently employs modified terms to signify an entirely separate category. Consider the term 'minor planet' - which the original draft sought to abolish - or even 'koala bear', which is a marsupial rather than a member of Ursidae.

The essential point is that classification must serve celestial mechanics. The straw poll figures reported yesterday simply demonstrate that the planetary scientists recognise this imperative. A body that cannot clear its orbital zone, or that is locked into an orbital resonance alongside thousands of other Kuiper belt objects, occupies a completely different physical regime than an isolated, dominant planet. Relegating Pluto to a dwarf class is not an insult or an act of malice; it is an acknowledgement of observational evidence. If the Executive Committee adopts the proposal put forward by Julio Angel Fernandez, astronomy will finally possess an objective, dynamical standard rather than a sentimental compromise.
