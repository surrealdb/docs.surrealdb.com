OffByOne wrote:
When a taxonomy reflects genuine physical boundaries, the surrounding terminology quickly becomes second nature.
That is very true. Scientific vocabulary often demands a modest period of cultural adjustment. When 19th-century astronomers eventually reclassified Ceres from a primary planet to an asteroid after realising the asteroid belt was populated by thousands of comparable rocky fragments, the broader public adjusted in due course.

Demanding that a planet govern its orbital domain simply recognises that the Kuiper belt represents an analogous dynamic population. We are not discarding Pluto, merely placing it alongside Ceres in a taxonomy that accurately describes the architecture of the solar system. Provided the plenary session adopts the text tomorrow, teachers and textbook authors will manage the transition reasonably well.

Categorisation is not merely clerical filing. A proper taxonomy reflects underlying physical mechanisms rather than arbitrary convenience. When an object fails to clear its orbital zone, that failure reveals the actual evolutionary history of the accretion disc, not just an administrative preference. Ceres and Pluto inhabit populated debris belts precisely because their mass was insufficient to sweep the surrounding space clear of competing bodies. Separating dominant planets from dwarf planets establishes a functional scientific distinction based on genuine celestial mechanics. The physical object certainly remains unaltered, but our theoretical framework becomes vastly more coherent.

First they spent years preparing a twelve-planet draft, then the moment that hit resistance they cobbled together three revisions in forty-eight hours, and now nobody outside that steering room even knows the precise text people have to vote up or down tomorrow afternoon. PintOfMild, I would not be so quick to toss seventy-six years of science out the window just yet... If this rushed compromise ends up rejected on the floor, the whole manufactured rush falls apart, and Pluto stays right where Clyde Tombaugh found it. We will see what happens when the actual ballots get counted tomorrow.

NewsThu Aug 24, 2006 8:24 am GMT · Prague, Czech RepublicFinal text: eight planets and a separate class of dwarf planetsrecalled by 5 members

Today's conference newspaper prints what Nature counts as version four, with a warning that only minor corrections are now possible. Resolution 5A defines a planet as a round body orbiting the Sun that has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit, and lists the eight from Mercury to Neptune. Round bodies that have not cleared their neighbourhood and are not satellites are 'dwarf planets'. 5B would call the eight 'classical planets'. 6A makes Pluto a dwarf planet and the prototype of a new class beyond Neptune, and 6B would name that class 'plutonian objects'. The vote is this afternoon. Source

NewsThu Aug 24, 2006 9:00 am GMT · Prague, Czech RepublicIAU: four votes this afternoon, by raised yellow cardsrecalled by 3 members

The IAU says members will vote on 5A, 5B, 6A and 6B in sequence at the session starting at 14:00 Prague time (12:00 UTC), with voting expected to end between 15:30 and 16:00 (13:30-14:00 UTC). Members present will raise yellow ballot cards, which will be counted. The term 'pluton' has been dropped after objections, especially from geologists. Ron Ekers says the IAU wants as many members as possible involved. A press conference is set for 18:00 (16:00 UTC). Source
