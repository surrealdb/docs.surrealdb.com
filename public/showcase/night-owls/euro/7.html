Actually, the tactile intaglio print and the foil elements were engineered specifically so that a cashier does not need prior muscle memory to detect them. The raised ink on the front produces a distinct rough texture against paper that is completely absent on standard photocopied or offset counterfeits. Furthermore, the notion that counterfeiters could already flood the market with indistinguishable replicas on day one ignores basic production logistics. Replicating the embedded iridescent stripes, varying watermark shades, and registration marks requires industrial machinery, not an overnight sprint. Taking three seconds to tilt a note towards the light is entirely standard practice, not a vulnerability.

kookaburra99 wrote:
exactly, feeling the rough ink and tilting it takes two seconds, so queues will be totally fine.
Small correction: one should not assume lines will vanish instantaneously, as brief administrative queues are entirely expected during any major monetary changeover. In 1971, Britain managed precisely such temporary transaction delays during decimalisation without commercial breakdown, as Punchcard noted.

Moreover, the vast majority of daily transactions do not require cashiers to calculate complex exchange rates in their heads. In the Netherlands, as elsewhere, point-of-sale terminals and cash registers were updated well in advance to compute dual totals automatically. Even where cashiers rely on printed conversion cards, verifying change takes mere seconds. Ordinary trade has accommodated new coinages for centuries; retail systems simply do not collapse over routine arithmetic.
